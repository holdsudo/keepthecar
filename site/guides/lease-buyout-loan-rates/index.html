Lease Buyout Loan Rates: What's Normal and What's a Rip-Off

A buyout loan is priced off the same grid as any used-car loan. Here is what moves the number, where the dealer hides its cut, and why a low payment is not a low rate.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

Why a buyout loan prices like a used-car loan

There is no lease buyout rate sheet. When a lender prices your buyout, it opens the used-car grid, because that is what the loan is: a used car, sold by a leasing company, to a buyer with a credit file. The fact that you have driven the car for three years and know every rattle in it does not appear anywhere on that grid.

So lease buyout loan rates follow used-car logic. Older car, higher rate. More miles, higher rate. Longer term, higher rate. Loan bigger than the car's book value, higher rate or no loan. Thinner credit, higher rate. Understanding that grid is the whole game, because every source that wants your buyout is going to hide one of those levers from you.

Credit tier

Lenders bucket you. The buckets have names like prime, near-prime and subprime, and each one has its own row on the grid. Moving up one bucket does more for your rate than anything else on this page. Your lease payment history counts, and three years of on-time payments to the leasing company is one of the better things in your file. A lender that does buyouts regularly knows to read it that way. A general used-car desk sees a score and a bucket. If your score is the problem, the bad credit guide covers what actually gets approved.

Term

Longer terms carry higher rates, and they carry them on more months. Both numbers go the wrong way at once. A 48-month loan and an 84-month loan on the same payoff are not the same product with different payments. The second one is a much more expensive loan on a car that will be eleven years old when you make the last payment.

Loan-to-value against book

The lender divides what you want to borrow by what its book says the car is worth. Under the cap, you get the normal grid. Over it, you get a higher row, a demand for cash down, or a decline. This is where fees matter. Say the residual is $18,000, hypothetically, and the book value is $21,000. Finance the residual alone and you are comfortably under book. Let a dealer add a doc fee, an inspection and two add-ons, roll in the tax, and you can cross the line, which moves you to a worse row on the grid for the entire term.

Equity works the other way. When the residual sits under market, which is the main reason people buy out a lease, the loan-to-value is good before you do anything, and a good loan-to-value is the one lever that can pull a mid-tier file into a better row.

Vehicle age and miles

Most lenders have a model-year cutoff and a mileage cutoff, and crossing either one moves you to a worse row or out of the program. You cannot change the odometer. You can change when you apply. A buyout that happens at the natural end of the lease sits where it sits. One that waits six months past a cutoff can cost a tier.

The dealer's rate has reserve in it

When the dealer arranges your loan, the lender tells the finance office the rate you qualified for. That is the buy rate. The dealer is allowed to write the contract at a higher rate and keep part of the difference as compensation. That is the reserve, and it is the single most profitable line in a dealer's buyout. It is also invisible. The contract shows one rate, and the dealer has no reason to show you the one underneath it.

The only reliable way to find out what your rate is without the reserve is to be approved somewhere the reserve does not exist. Then the dealer's number is a quote you can read, instead of a number you have to trust.

The rule. A rate is only comparable when the amount financed, the term and the fees are identical. Change any one of the three and the lower rate can be the more expensive loan.

The low payment at 72 or 84 months

This is the trap, and it is the same trap at the dealer and at the big online outfits. You ask for a rate and get a payment. The payment is low. The payment is low because the term is 72 or 84 months, and the rate for that term is higher than the rate for 48 or 60.

Walk it through without any invented numbers. The car has three years on it already. An 84-month loan takes it to ten. Somewhere around year five or six, the car's value falls under what you still owe, because the long term is paying down principal slowly while the car depreciates at its own pace. That is negative equity, and it is the reason the dealer loves this structure: the next time you walk in, you cannot leave without rolling that shortfall into a new lease. The volume shops like it for a different reason. Their processing fee is inside the loan, and a longer term means more months of interest on it.

Ask for the rate at each term, in writing, and ask for the total of payments at each term. The total is the number that tells the truth. The payment is the number that sells.

How fees in the loan change the real cost

Every fee that gets financed is money you pay interest on. The purchase-option fee and the sales tax are real costs, and financing them is usually sensible, because the alternative is finding that cash on the day the payoff letter expires. The doc fee, the inspection fee, the online outfit's service fee and the add-ons are not real costs, and financing them means paying interest on padding for the life of the loan. Which fees are real is sorted out in the fees guide.

Hypothetically, if a lender advertises a rate a bit lower than a specialist's but adds a flat fee into the amount financed, and the specialist charges nothing extra, the lower advertised rate can cost more over the term. You cannot see that by comparing rates. You can only see it by comparing the amount financed and the total of payments.

How to get the best rate without getting played

  1. Pull your own payoff letter from the leasing company so you know the real amount before anyone quotes you.
  2. Check your credit yourself before applying. Fix what is fixable, and keep hard inquiries to one, which means one application, handled privately, not a form that goes out to a panel.
  3. Ask for the rate and the total of payments at 48, 60 and 72 months, side by side.
  4. Ask what is in the amount financed beyond residual, purchase-option fee, tax and title. Anything else is negotiable.
  5. Compare that against a lender that does the work. The loans guide explains why the APR alone is the wrong question.

The short version

Lease buyout loan rates come off the used-car grid: credit tier, term, loan-to-value, age and miles. Equity in the car is the lever most people have and most lenders ignore.

The dealer's rate has reserve in it, the 84-month payment is a trap, and every financed fee is interest on nothing. Compare amount financed and total of payments, not the rate alone.

Quick answers

Are lease buyout interest rates the same as used-car rates?

Essentially, yes. Lenders price a buyout off their used-car grid, by credit tier, term, loan-to-value, and the car's age and miles. The one edge a buyout has is equity, when the residual sits under market value.

Why is the dealer's rate higher than what I was approved for?

Because the dealer can mark up the lender's buy rate and keep part of the difference. The contract shows only the marked-up rate. Getting approved somewhere without that markup is the only way to see the gap.

Is a longer term worth it for a lower payment?

Rarely. Longer terms carry higher rates on more months, and the car will likely be worth less than you owe for years. Ask for the total of payments at each term and compare that number, not the payment.
