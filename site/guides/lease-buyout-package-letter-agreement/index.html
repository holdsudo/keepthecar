Lease Buyout Package, Payoff Letter, Agreement: Every Document Explained

A buyout package is the leasing company's side of the sale: a price with a deadline and the forms the state wants. Here is what every page does and which pages are not really part of it.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

What a lease buyout package is

When you tell the leasing company you want to buy the car, it sends a set of documents. People call it the buyout package, the purchase package, or the payoff package. The name changes by company. The contents mostly do not.

The package is the leasing company's side of the sale. It states the price, tells you how to pay, and gives you the forms the state will want when the title moves to your name. That is all it is. It is not a loan. It is not an approval. It is a quote with a deadline and a stack of paper.

Most of the trouble people have with a lease buyout agreement comes from not knowing which page does what. So here is every document, what each line means, and which ones you can safely ignore.

The payoff or purchase letter

This is the only page with a number on it that matters. Some companies call it a lease buyout letter, some call it a purchase quote. It shows the residual from your contract, any remaining payments if you are buying early, the purchase-option fee if your contract names one, and any late charges or unpaid amounts on the account. It adds them up and prints a total. Then it prints a date.

Read the date first. A payoff is good for a short window, often ten days or so, because interest and payments keep moving. After the date, the number is void and you ask for a new letter. Everything you do next has to land inside that window.

Ask for the letter in your own name, from the leasing company, not from the dealer. The dealer's version of the same number has the dealer's fees mixed in. The leasing company's letter is clean.

The buyout agreement or bill of sale

This is the sale contract. Some companies send it as a lease buyout agreement form, some as a bill of sale, some as a purchase option exercise notice. It names the car by VIN, names the price from the payoff letter, names you as buyer, and says the car is sold as-is. You sign it and send it back.

Check three things. The VIN matches your car. The price matches the payoff letter. The buyer's name matches the name you want on the title. If a co-lessee is on the lease, their name is usually required on this form too. A missing signature here is the most common reason a package bounces back.

The odometer disclosure statement

Federal law requires an odometer disclosure when a title changes hands on most vehicles. The leasing company is the seller, so it has to disclose the mileage, and you have to acknowledge it. The package includes the form. You write the current mileage, sign, and return it. Do not estimate. Walk out to the car and read the dash. A mileage that conflicts with a later DMV reading can stall the title for weeks.

The power of attorney for title

Some packages include a limited power of attorney. It lets a named party sign title documents on the leasing company's behalf, or on yours, so the title can be processed without a company officer in the room. It is limited to this one transaction and this one car. Read the scope, make sure it names only the vehicle, and sign it if the state or the leasing company requires it. It is how the title gets signed when the seller is a corporation in another state.

Payment instructions

The last page tells you how to pay: wire details, an overnight address for a cashier's check, sometimes an online payment option. It says who is allowed to pay. That line matters. Some companies accept funds from the lessee, some from a lender on the lessee's behalf, some only through a dealer. Nobody can tell you the current rule except the company itself. Ask, and get the answer in writing with the package.

Expiration and the clock

The whole package runs on the payoff letter's date. Inside that window you need the money in hand, the signed agreement and odometer statement back to the leasing company, and the funds sent by a method they accept. If any one of those is late, you start over with a fresh letter and sometimes a fresh signature set.

This is why the people who run buyouts for a living pull the payoff last, not first. Line up the financing, confirm the paperwork, then request the letter and send the money within days. Doing it in the other order is how a ten-day letter expires while a loan officer is on vacation.

The mistakes that stall it

  • A payment sent after the expiration date, or for the old amount.
  • A co-lessee who did not sign the agreement or the odometer statement.
  • Mileage on the odometer form that does not match the car.
  • A buyer name on the agreement that does not match the name on the loan or the insurance.
  • Funds sent by a method the instructions do not allow, such as a personal check when the letter asks for a wire.
  • Pages returned by email when the company requires wet signatures by mail, or the reverse.

Each one costs a week or two. None of them is hard to avoid if you read the package before you sign it. For what happens after the payment clears, see how the title actually gets to you.

What the dealer adds that you do not need

If you let the dealer handle the buyout, the leasing company's package gets buried under the dealer's own paperwork. A buyer's order with a doc fee. A retail installment contract with the dealer's rate on it. An inspection sheet for a car you already own in every way but name. Often a menu of add-ons from the finance office.

None of that is a lease buyout agreement. The agreement is the leasing company's form, and the leasing company's price is on the payoff letter. The dealer's stack is the dealer getting paid for a sale it did not want. The four traps on the homepage explain why. If you want to know whether the price itself can move before you sign, read how to ask a leasing company about its buyout rule.

The one-line rule. If a page did not come from the leasing company, it is not part of your buyout package, and you can ask why you are signing it.

What to do with this

  1. Call the leasing company and ask for the purchase package in your name. Ask who is allowed to send the payoff.
  2. Read the payoff letter. Note the total and the expiration date.
  3. Check the agreement for VIN, price, and buyer name. Get every signer on the lease to sign.
  4. Read the real mileage off the dash and put it on the odometer statement.
  5. Send the funds by the method the instructions name, before the date, and keep the confirmation.

That is the whole package. The full start-to-finish timeline shows where it sits in the rest of the process.

The short version

A lease buyout package is five documents from the leasing company: the payoff letter, the buyout agreement, the odometer statement, sometimes a power of attorney, and the payment instructions. Read the expiration date before anything else.

Anything the dealer adds on top is not part of the package. It is the dealer's paperwork, and it is where the extra money goes.

Quick answers

What is a lease buyout package?

It is the set of documents the leasing company sends when you ask to buy the car: a payoff letter with the price and an expiration date, a buyout agreement or bill of sale, an odometer statement, sometimes a limited power of attorney, and payment instructions.

How long is a lease buyout letter good for?

A short window, often around ten days, because payments and interest keep moving. The letter prints its own expiration date. After that date you need a new letter, so line up the money before you request it.

Do I need the dealer to process the buyout agreement?

The agreement comes from the leasing company and is signed by you. Whether the company requires a dealer to be involved in the payoff is its own rule, and it changes. Ask the leasing company directly and get the answer in writing with the package.
