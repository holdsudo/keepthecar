Taxes: California

California Sales Tax on a Lease Buyout: The DMV Does the Collecting

California taxes the buyout of a leased car as a purchase, at the combined rate for the address where the car is registered, and the DMV collects it when the title moves to you. The tax you paid during the lease covered the payments, not the car.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

How much sales tax do I pay on the buyout of a leased car in California?

The buyout price, times the combined sales and use tax rate for the place the car is registered. That is the formula. The statewide base is 7.25%, and on top of it sit district taxes set by counties, cities and transit districts, so in the big counties the combined rate often lands at 9 to 10 percent or more. The rate is set by your registered address, not by where the leasing company is or where the dealer sits.

Technically this is use tax rather than sales tax, because the leasing company is not a California retailer collecting at a register; it is a sale to you on which you owe the tax. The distinction changes nothing about the amount. It is assessed on the buyout price at your local combined rate and paid to the DMV when the car is titled in your name.

Look up the combined rate for your city on the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration site before you do any math. District rates change, and the only figure worth building on is the one the CDTFA publishes for your address.

Why the tax you already paid does not count

During the lease, California taxed your monthly payments. The leasing company owned the car, you paid to use it, and the state taxed the use. That money is gone and it bought what it bought.

At the end of the lease the leasing company sells you the car. That is a new transaction with a new price, and California taxes it on its own. There is no credit for the lease-period tax, no netting, no partial rate. People who paid tax on every payment for three years and then find the full rate on the buyout price feel cheated. The state's position is that they were taxed on two different things, and the state is the one holding the title.

Who collects it, and when

Do the buyout yourself and the California DMV collects the tax when you transfer the title into your name. You bring the title and lien release from the leasing company, the paperwork showing the buyout price, and the money. The DMV applies the rate for your registered address and will not release the title until it is paid.

Go through a dealer and the dealer collects the tax on the buyer's order and remits it. The tax is the same number. The sheet around it is not: a doc fee, an inspection of the car you have been driving, add-ons, and a loan rate with the dealer's compensation inside it. The tax is the one line the state wrote. The dealer's own lines are covered on the homepage under the four traps.

A specialist pays the tax to the DMV as part of transferring the title and finances it inside the loan. You never take a number at the DMV.

The narrow exception: buy, then sell within ten days

California has a rule that can spare the use tax on a lease buyout if you buy the car out and resell it within ten days of the buyout, because the transaction is treated as a purchase for resale rather than a purchase for your own use. It exists for people whose plan is to flip the equity: buy the car at the residual, sell it to a retailer or a private buyer for more, and keep the difference without paying tax on a car they never intended to keep.

It is narrow, it has conditions and paperwork, and the timing is unforgiving. Do not build a plan on it from a forum post. Confirm the current rule, the exact conditions, and what documentation the DMV and the CDTFA want, with the CDTFA directly, before you count on it. If the resale slips past the window, or the buyer is not the kind the rule contemplates, the tax is owed in full. The buyers who typically sit on the other side of that trade, the big used-car retailers, are covered in Carvana and CarMax lease buyouts.

The rule. California use tax on a lease buyout is the buyout price times the combined rate for your registered address, 7.25% base plus district taxes, paid to the DMV when the title moves. The only way around it is the ten-day resale rule, and only if the CDTFA confirms you qualify.

Third-party buyouts: there is no right to one in California

A related question people ask alongside the tax one: can someone else, a retailer or a friend or a lender, buy the car straight out of the lease to avoid the tax landing on you? California law does not give the lessee or anyone else a right to a third-party buyout. Whether a leasing company will sell to anyone but the lessee is the leasing company's policy, and it changes. Ask yours for the current rule, in writing, before anybody makes a plan around it. The mechanics and the traps are in third-party lease buyout.

Why financing it matters

At a 10% combined rate on a $20,000 residual the tax is $2,000, due the day the title moves, against a payoff letter that expires in days. A big bank or credit union lends against a book value and tells you the tax is yours. A dealer finances it at a rate you cannot see inside. A specialist finances residual, purchase-option fee and tax as one loan and pays the DMV. Before anyone pulls your credit, get one answer in writing: is the California use tax in the loan amount?

What to do with this

  1. Get the payoff letter from the leasing company. Note the price, the purchase-option fee, and the expiration date.
  2. Look up the combined rate for your registered address on the CDTFA site.
  3. Multiply the buyout price by that rate. Add the DMV title and transfer charges. That is your real number.
  4. If your plan is to sell the car rather than keep it, ask the CDTFA about the ten-day rule before you buy, not after.
  5. Decide who pays the tax, you or the loan, before the letter expires. Get it in writing.

The short version

California charges use tax on a lease buyout at the combined rate for the address where the car is registered: 7.25% statewide plus district taxes, so often 9 to 10 percent or more in the big counties. The DMV collects it when the title moves to you, and the tax paid during the lease does not reduce it.

Confirm your rate with the CDTFA, confirm the ten-day resale rule with the CDTFA if you plan to sell, and do not sign a loan that leaves the tax for you to find in cash.

Quick answers

How much sales tax do I pay on the buyout of a leased car in California?

The buyout price times the combined rate for your registered address: 7.25% statewide base plus your district taxes, which often puts big-county drivers at 9 to 10 percent or more. Confirm your exact rate on the CDTFA site.

I paid tax on every lease payment. Why does California tax the buyout?

The tax on the payments was on your use of a car the leasing company owned. The buyout is a separate sale of the car to you, and California taxes it on its own. There is no credit for what you paid during the lease.

Can I avoid California tax on a lease buyout if I sell the car right away?

There is a narrow rule that can spare the use tax if you resell within ten days of the buyout. It has conditions and paperwork, so confirm the current rule with the CDTFA before you rely on it. Miss the window and the full tax is owed.
