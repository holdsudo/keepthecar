Lease Then Buy Out vs Finance From Day One

Both paths end with you owning the same car. One pays tax, title, fees, and interest once. The other pays most of them twice. Here is how to add it up and when the second path still wins.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

The question, stated honestly

You are choosing between two ways to end up owning the same car. One is a loan from day one: you buy it, you pay it off, you own it. The other is a lease now and a buyout later: you rent it for three years, then buy it at the residual, usually with a second loan. The second path is what most people mean by lease to buy.

The lease then buyout vs finance question has a real answer, but it is not the one the salesperson gives. The dealer likes the lease because it puts you back in the showroom in three years. The big online volume shops like the buyout because it is a second loan to write. Neither has a reason to show you the whole bill.

The framework: add up every dollar on each path

Both paths end at the same place, you holding the title, so the only honest comparison is total cash out of your pocket from day one to that day. Put every line on paper. The small lines are where the lease path loses.

Cost lineFinance from day oneLease, then buy out
Price paid for the carNegotiated price, onceDepreciation during the lease, then the residual
Interest or rent chargeOne loan, one rateMoney factor on the lease, then a rate on the buyout loan
Sales taxOnce, on the price, in most statesOn the payments, then again on the residual, in most states
Acquisition and purchase-option feesNoneBoth, if the contract names them
Title and registrationOnceTwice: at lease start and again at buyout
Loan or doc feesOne setTwo sets, one per transaction
Mileage and wear chargesNoneNone if you buy; they vanish on a buyout

Two things jump out of that table. The lease path pays tax, title, and fees twice. And it pays finance charges on two separate contracts. Those are the structural costs of the lease path.

Tax deserves a note. Most states tax lease payments as you make them and then tax the buyout as a new sale. A few states tax a lease differently, up front or on the full value. The rate and the method are set by your state, so confirm both with the state's tax or motor vehicle agency before you count on any number.

A hypothetical, clearly labeled

These numbers are invented to show the mechanics. They are not an average or a quote. Use your own contract.

Say the car sells for $32,000 and you can finance the whole price. Say the same car leases for 36 months with $2,000 due at signing and $450 a month, and the residual printed in the lease is $18,000. Set interest aside for a moment and just add up the principal and the fixed costs.

  • Finance from day one: $32,000 for the car. Tax once on $32,000. Title and registration once. One set of loan fees.
  • Lease, then buy out: $2,000 at signing, plus 36 payments of $450, which is $16,200, plus the $18,000 residual. That is $36,200 before any fee. Tax on the $16,200 of payments and then tax again on the $18,000 residual. Title and registration twice. An acquisition fee at the start and a purchase-option fee at the end, if the contract names them.

In this made-up example the lease path pays $4,200 more in principal before interest, fees, and the second round of tax. Then interest lands on both sides: one rate on the $32,000 loan versus the lease's money factor on the depreciation plus a second rate on the $18,000 buyout loan. Whether that gap grows or shrinks depends entirely on the rates you actually get, which is why you run it with your own numbers and not these. The buyout calculation guide shows how to build the buyout side line by line.

When lease then buy wins

Lease to buy is not always the expensive path. It wins in a few specific situations, and you can usually see them coming.

The lease carried a subsidized money factor

Manufacturers sometimes push a model with a very low lease rate. If the money factor on your lease was lower than the loan rate you would have paid, you borrowed the depreciation cheaply for three years. That can offset the second set of fees.

The residual ends up below market

The residual was set three years ago by someone guessing at the used-car market. If the car is now worth more than the residual, you buy it at a discount the leasing company cannot take back. Residuals are contractual and leasing companies rarely move them. In that case the buyout is the cheapest way to own that car.

You genuinely did not know

If three years ago you were not sure you would keep the car, the lease bought you an option. The option cost money, in fees and the second round of tax. If you used it, and you now want the car, the cost was the price of not guessing wrong. The keep-or-return guide is where that decision gets made.

When it loses

It loses in the ordinary case. The money factor was average, the residual came out near market, and you always knew you would keep the car. Then the lease path is the financed path with two extra sets of fees, two rounds of tax in most states, and two finance charges instead of one. The low monthly payment during the lease was not savings. It was deferral, and the residual is the bill.

It also loses when the second loan is bad. A lease buyout loan on a three-year-old car is priced as a used-car loan, often with a shorter term and a higher rate than the new-car loan you turned down. If the dealer arranges it, the rate has the dealer's compensation inside it. If a volume shop arranges it, there is a processing fee rolled into the principal. The rate guide covers what a fair buyout loan looks like.

What neither side tells you

The dealer will compare monthly payments, because the lease payment is lower and the comparison ends there. Ask instead for the total of payments on each path through the day you own the car. The dealer has that number.

The big bank and the credit union will quote you a loan rate for the buyout and nothing else. They will not finance the second round of tax or the fees, so the lease path costs cash at the end that the day-one loan would have folded in. Count that cash as part of the comparison. The fee guide lists which charges are real at the buyout and which are padding.

The one-line rule. If you know today that you will keep the car, finance it once; the lease path only wins when the lease rate was subsidized or the residual turns out to be below market.

What to do with this

  1. Write both paths out on one page, every line in the table above, with your own numbers.
  2. Get the state's tax treatment of leases and buyouts from the state agency, not from the dealer.
  3. If you are already in the lease, compare the residual to what the car sells for today. That decides most of it.
  4. Price the buyout loan before the payoff letter is pulled, so the second financing does not become the second trap.

The short version

Financing from day one is cheaper in the ordinary case because the lease path pays tax, title, fees, and finance charges twice. The low lease payment is deferral, not savings.

Lease then buy wins only when the lease carried a subsidized rate, the residual landed below market, or you honestly did not know whether you would keep the car. Run both paths on one page with your own numbers before anyone shows you a monthly payment.

Quick answers

Is it cheaper to lease and then buy or to finance from the start?

Usually financing from the start, because the lease path pays sales tax, title, fees, and finance charges on two transactions instead of one. The lease path can win when the lease rate was subsidized or the residual ends up below the car's market value.

Do I pay sales tax twice if I lease and then buy the car?

In most states, yes. Tax is charged on the lease payments as you make them and then on the buyout price as a new sale. A few states treat leases differently, so confirm the method and rate with your state's tax or motor vehicle agency.

When does a lease then buyout make sense?

When the residual printed in your lease is below what the car is worth today, when the lease carried an unusually low money factor, or when you were not sure three years ago that you would keep the car and the lease bought you that option.
