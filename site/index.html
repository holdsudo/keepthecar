Your lease is ending. Here's who not to hand it to.

Four places take most lease buyouts: the dealer, a big bank, a credit union, and a big online lease end outfit. Each one has a built-in reason to make your buyout cost more, take longer, or both. Here's what each actually does, and who is worth a call instead.

About a 6 minute read. No ads, no form, nothing to sign.

The four traps

Your buyout is three numbers: the residual, the fees, and sales tax. Everyone below either adds to those numbers, refuses to finance some of them, or hands you the paperwork and walks away.

Trap 1

The dealer

The showroom where you leased it, or any franchised store.

The dealer wants one of two outcomes: your car back on the lot at the residual so they can resell it at market, or you in a new lease. A buyout that leaves you owning the car at the leasing company's price is the least profitable thing that can happen in that building, so they make up for it.

Expect a doc fee for paperwork the leasing company would have done for free, a purchase-option fee, an "inspection," and whatever add-ons make it onto the buyer's order. If the dealer arranges the loan, understand how that works: lenders compensate dealers for placing loans, so the rate on the contract is the rate you qualified for plus the dealer's cut. You will not see the two numbers separately.

Verdict: the most expensive room you can do this in. Get your payoff letter from the leasing company directly and never let the dealer "handle it."
Trap 2

The big national bank

The one your checking account is with.

A big bank treats a lease buyout as a used-car loan on a car it has never seen. It pulls a book value, applies a hard loan-to-value cap, and compares that to your payoff. Over the cap? Bring cash. Negative equity? Declined. The disposition fee, the purchase-option fee, the sales tax? Not financed, those are yours too.

Then the part nobody mentions: the bank doesn't pull your payoff, doesn't chase the odometer statement, doesn't send the funds on the day the payoff letter expires, doesn't handle title, doesn't register the car. You get a check and a to-do list, and a payoff letter that was good for ten days.

Verdict: a rigid loan from an institution that has no idea what a lease buyout is. Fine if your car is well under book, you have cash for every fee, and you enjoy the DMV.
Trap 3

The credit union

"Just go to a credit union, they have the best rates."

The rate can be good. That is the whole case, and it is the only part of the process a credit union is good at. First you join, which means an account, a deposit, and sometimes an association membership you've never heard of. Then you wait for a loan officer who does three of these a year.

The approval comes with the same loan-to-value cap and the same "we don't finance fees or tax" as the bank, plus a used-car pricing tier that gets worse the moment your car is four years old or crosses the mileage line. A surprising number will not fund a third-party buyout at all; they'll tell you the leasing company has to be the seller and then discover it isn't, two weeks in. Nobody at the credit union pulls your payoff, tracks its expiration, wires the funds, or touches the title. If the loan officer is out the week your payoff letter expires, that's your problem.

Verdict: they don't help. They approve a number and leave you to run the entire buyout yourself, on a clock they don't watch. Good rate, no help, and the fees and tax come out of your pocket.
Trap 4

The big online lease end outfit

The ones with the ad budget.

Volume shops. Thousands of buyouts a month, a call-center phone tree, and a lender panel that spits back a rate. You are a ticket number. Read the fee line before anything else, because a flat "processing" or "service" fee is where the margin lives, and it gets rolled into the loan so you pay interest on it for six years. Watch the term too: the "low monthly payment" is the dealer's oldest trick stretched to 72 or 84 months on a car you've already driven for three years.

The tell is simple. If you cannot get the same human on the phone twice, nobody there owns your file.

Verdict: convenient until something goes wrong, and something goes wrong on a lot of buyouts. Bigger is not better here; bigger is a queue.

What a real buyout partner actually does

Hold anyone you talk to against this list. Every "no" is a job that lands back on you.

  • Tells you the number before you apply. Residual, fees, sales tax, and where the car sits against market value, so you know whether buying it out is even the right call.
  • Pulls the payoff letter and watches its expiration. Payoff quotes are good for days, not weeks. Somebody has to own the clock.
  • Finances the whole buyout. Residual plus fees plus tax. Not "the residual, and bring a check for the rest."
  • One application, handled privately. Your credit should not be shotgunned to a dozen lenders by a form.
  • Sends the funds, then handles title, registration, and plates. You should not learn what "lien release" means at a DMV window.
  • A human who calls you back. The same one, with your file open.

Who is actually worth a call

Three names, three different jobs. Each one does one thing and says so. None of them pretends to do the other two. That is the whole test.

One more to skip: anything Canadian.

Search "lease buyout" and you will also see Canadian sites like Clutch and Leasebusters. They are real companies, but they only handle Canadian leases. They cannot pay off a US lease or title a US car.

If the site mentions Ontario or British Columbia, it is not for you. Close the tab.

Quick answers

Can I buy out my lease with any lender I want?

Usually, but not always. Some leasing companies only accept payoff from you or from a franchised dealer, and some refuse third-party buyouts entirely. Get the leasing company's third-party policy in writing before anyone pulls your credit.

Why is the dealer's buyout number higher than my payoff letter?

Because the dealer padded it. The payoff letter is the leasing company's number. The dealer's number is that plus a doc fee for paperwork that was already done, a purchase-option fee, an "inspection" of a car they leased you, and whatever add-ons they slipped onto the buyer's order. If they arrange the loan, the rate has their cut baked in and you will never see it. The dealer did not want this sale, so they are making you pay for it.

Isn't a credit union loan good enough?

No. The rate is the only thing a credit union gets right, and it comes with a catch on every other line. You join first. You wait for a loan officer who does three buyouts a year. The approval caps the loan at a book value that ignores your payoff, refuses the fees and the sales tax, and may refuse the whole thing because the seller is a leasing company. Then nobody there pulls your payoff, watches its expiration, wires the funds, or touches the title. A good rate on a loan you have to run yourself is not help.

Can't I just do the buyout myself?

You can, and it is a part-time job. Get the payoff letter and notice it expires in ten days. Line up the money in that window. Chase the odometer statement and the lien release. Learn which forms your state wants, in what order, and find out at the DMV window that one of them is wrong. Pay the sales tax in cash. Do it all on your lunch break, because every office you need is only open when you are at work. People who pay cash and have time do this. Everyone else is better off handing it to someone who does it every day.
