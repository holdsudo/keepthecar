Over Your Lease Miles? Buy the Car or Pay the Per-Mile Bill

Over your miles, returning the car means a bill per mile. Buying it means no bill, but you own the lost value. Here is how to tell which costs less.

Published October 28, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

The bill that shows up after the keys are gone

Every lease sets a mileage allowance and a charge for each mile over it. Both numbers are printed in your contract. Return the car over the allowance and the leasing company bills you for every extra mile, usually weeks after the inspection, when the car is already gone and you have nothing left to bargain with.

Buy the car instead and that bill never gets written. Nobody reads the odometer to charge you. The miles do not vanish, though. They come off what the car is worth, and once you own it, that loss is yours. So the real question is simple: which version of the miles costs you less.

That is the whole lease buyout over mileage decision. It is arithmetic, not a feeling, and you can do it in ten minutes with your contract and a pricing guide.

Find your three numbers

  1. Your overage. Read the odometer today. Estimate where it will be on the turn-in date. Subtract the total allowance in the contract. Most leases state the allowance per year, so multiply by the years in the term.
  2. Your per-mile charge. It sits in the section on excess wear and mileage. Use the number in the contract, not a figure someone quotes you on the phone.
  3. Your payoff and the car's value. The payoff comes from the leasing company, in writing, good through a date. The value comes from the pricing guides at your actual mileage: private-party and trade-in, not the dealer's opinion.

Multiply the first number by the second and you have the return bill for miles alone. Add the disposition fee and any wear charges you expect. That is the full cost of walking away. The buyout calculation guide walks through the payoff side line by line.

A worked example

These numbers are hypothetical. They show the math, not your result.

Say your lease allowed 36,000 miles and the car will show 48,000 at turn-in. That is 12,000 miles over. Say the contract charges $0.25 a mile. The mileage bill is $3,000. Add a hypothetical $395 disposition fee and returning the car costs $3,395 before anyone looks at the bumpers.

Now say the payoff, purchase-option fee included, is $21,000, and the pricing guides put the car at $20,000 private-party with 48,000 miles on it. On paper the car is $1,000 underwater. Most people stop reading there and return it.

Return the carBuy the car
Mileage charge$3,000None
Disposition fee$395None
Gap between payoff and valueNot your problemAbout $1,000
What you have afterwardNo carA car worth about $20,000
Net cost of the miles$3,395About $1,000

In this example buying is cheaper by roughly $2,400, even though the car is worth less than the payoff. An underwater car with a big mileage bill can still be the cheaper door.

Sales tax is the honest complication. You pay it on the buyout. But if you return the car, you will pay tax on whatever you drive next, so compare like with like: a car you keep against a car you would have bought anyway.

Over your miles, price the return bill first. If it is bigger than the gap between payoff and value, the miles are telling you to buy.

When paying the bill is the better move

  • The overage is small. A few hundred miles over is a small bill. Do not buy a car you do not want to dodge it.
  • The car is deep underwater. If the payoff exceeds the value by more than the whole return bill, returning is cheaper. Run the numbers, do not guess.
  • You do not want this car for the next few years. High miles now means maintenance sooner. Check where the car sits against the manufacturer's warranty, which ends at a mileage limit as well as an age limit. A car past its basic coverage is a different purchase than one inside it.
  • You would sell it right away. Buying only to flip it adds tax, title and a sale to the math. Sometimes it still wins. Often the margin disappears.

If you are on the fence about keeping the car at all, the should I buy out my lease guide covers the decision beyond the miles.

The traps on the way out

The dealer who makes your miles disappear

A dealer may offer to "take care of" the overage if you lease or buy your next car there. The charge does not disappear. Somebody still pays it, and the cost tends to move into the numbers on your next deal, where it is much harder to see. Ask for the new deal priced with and without the favor. If the two prices are the same, the favor was free. They are rarely the same.

The dealer who buys your car out from under you

The same high-mileage car that scares you may look fine to a dealer, who buys it at the payoff and sells it for more. If the miles are a reason for a dealer to want it, they are a reason for you to run the numbers again.

Prepaying miles you will never be billed for

Some contracts let you buy extra miles during the lease, often at a lower rate than the overage charge. Whether yours does, and at what price, is a question for the leasing company, and the answer should come in writing. If there is any chance you will buy the car, prepaid miles are money spent on a bill you were never going to receive. Ask whether unused prepaid miles are refunded at a purchase before you pay for them.

The processing fee that eats the saving

The math above only works if the buyout itself is clean. A dealer doc fee or a volume shop's processing fee can wipe out the margin. The lease buyout fees guide sorts the real charges from the padding.

Doing the buyout once you decide

Ask the leasing company for a written payoff good through a specific date, with the purchase-option fee shown. Ask for the current rule on a buyout through an outside lender, in writing, before you assume anything. Then size the loan to the payoff plus tax and title, not the payoff alone, or you will be finding the tax in cash at the DMV.

Tell any lender the real mileage up front. A lender may price or limit loans on higher-mileage cars, and you want that answer before you apply, not after.

The short version

Over your miles, the return bill is a real cost and the buyout makes it go away. Compare that bill with the gap between your payoff and the car's value at its actual mileage.

If the bill is bigger, buying is usually the cheaper door, even on a car that is slightly underwater. If the gap is bigger, or you do not want the car, pay the bill and walk.

Quick answers

Do I pay the excess mileage charge if I buy out my lease?

Mileage charges are return charges, so a purchase normally avoids them. Confirm it by reading the excess mileage section of your contract and checking that the written payoff shows no mileage line.

Does high mileage change my buyout price?

The buyout price is the residual set in your contract, and leasing companies rarely move it. High mileage lowers what the car is worth, not what you pay for it.

Can a dealer waive my over-mileage charge?

A dealer can offer to cover it as part of a new deal, but the cost usually moves into that deal. Ask for the new deal priced with and without the offer and compare.
