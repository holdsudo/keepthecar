How a Lease Buyout Works, Start to Finish

A lease buyout is you paying the leasing company the price in your contract and taking the title. Here is the whole process in order, who does each step, and where it goes wrong.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

What a lease buyout is

A lease is a long rental with a price tag at the end. The leasing company owns the car. You pay for the part of its value you use up, plus a finance charge. At the end you can hand it back or buy it. The lease buyout meaning is just that second choice: you pay the leasing company the price written in your contract, and the car becomes yours.

The price is the residual value, set the day you signed, plus a purchase-option fee if your contract names one, plus sales tax in most states, plus title and registration. If you buy early, remaining payments and any early termination charge get added. That is what is a lease buyout, in one paragraph. So how does a lease buyout work in practice? The rest of this guide is the process step by step, and who does what.

About 90 days out: decide

Ninety days before the lease ends is when the leasing company starts mailing you. The letters push a return and a new lease. Ignore the push and do the arithmetic. Pull the residual from your contract. Look up what the car sells for today. If the car is worth more than the residual, the buyout is a discount. If it is worth less, you are paying above market to keep a car you know. Either answer can be right, but you need to know which one you are choosing.

This is also when you ask the leasing company one question: who is allowed to pay off this lease, and does the company require a dealer to be involved. The answer changes by company and over time. Get it in writing. Do not take the dealer's word for it, because the dealer's answer is always that the dealer must be involved.

The payoff letter

The payoff letter is the leasing company's price with an expiration date. It shows the residual, the purchase-option fee, any remaining payments, and a total good for a short window. Request it when the money is ready, not before, because a letter that expires while you are still arranging a loan has to be requested again. The buyout package guide explains every page that comes with it.

Arranging the money

Cash is simplest and rare. Everyone else takes a loan. The loan has to cover the residual, the fee, the tax, and the title charges, or you are bringing the difference in cash. A big bank or a credit union lends against book value and leaves the tax and fees to you. A dealer lends with its compensation built into the rate. A volume shop rolls a processing fee into the principal. The loan guide walks through what a buyout loan should and should not look like. Whoever lends, the funds need to be ready inside the payoff letter's window.

Paying the leasing company

The money goes to the leasing company by the method the payoff letter names, usually a wire or an overnight cashier's check, for the exact amount, before the date. With it go the signed buyout agreement and the odometer statement. If a lender is paying, the lender sends the funds and the leasing company notes the lender as the party to receive the title. The account closes when the payment posts.

Title transfer

The leasing company then releases its lien and sends the title to you, or to your lender, on paper or through the state's electronic system. This takes weeks. Nobody at a bank or credit union watches it. The title transfer guide covers what arrives, when, and what to do when it does not.

Registration and plates

With the title released, the car gets titled and registered in your name at the state agency. Sales tax on the buyout is usually paid here. In many states you keep your plates. Bring the bill of sale, the odometer statement, the lien release, and the tax. Or have the person running your buyout do it, which is what a real buyout partner does as part of the job.

Insurance

Your policy listed the leasing company as loss payee. The week the payoff goes out, call the insurer, remove the leasing company, and add the new lender if there is one. Keep the coverage levels your lender requires. Leases usually required higher limits than a loan does, so this is also the moment to re-check the policy.

Where the four traps show up

The four traps on the homepage each appear at a specific step.

  • The dealer appears at the decision step, with a letter offering to "handle" the buyout, and again at the money step, with a loan that carries its cut. Its doc fee, inspection, and add-ons all land at the payment step.
  • The big bank appears at the money step and vanishes after it. The tax and fees are not financed, and the title and registration are yours to run.
  • The credit union appears at the money step with a good rate, a membership to join, a loan-to-value cap, and no one watching the payoff clock.
  • The big online lease-end outfit appears at the money step with a processing fee in the principal and a long term, and at the title step with a ticket number instead of a person.

How long it takes

From the first phone call to a title in your name, plan on four to eight weeks. The decision takes as long as you give it. Financing takes days to a couple of weeks depending on who is doing it. The payoff letter is good for roughly ten days, and the payment should go out within it. The title takes two to four weeks after payment, and the state agency adds whatever its calendar adds.

The step that blows up the schedule is the payoff letter expiring while the money is not ready. Everything after that is a reset. Line up the loan first, then pull the letter, then pay. That order is the whole trick.

The one-line rule. A lease buyout is a purchase with a deadline: the payoff letter sets the price and the clock, and everything else is arranged to land inside it.

What to do with this

  1. At 90 days, compare the residual in your contract to what the car sells for today.
  2. Ask the leasing company, in writing, who can pay off the lease and whether a dealer is required.
  3. Line up a loan that covers the residual, fees, and tax, before the payoff letter is pulled.
  4. Request the letter, send the exact amount by the named method, inside the window.
  5. Follow the title, register the car, and update the insurance.

That is how do lease buyouts work when they go right. When they go wrong, it is almost always because someone skipped step two or reversed steps three and four.

The short version

A lease buyout is you paying the leasing company the residual, plus the fee, tax, and title charges, and the title moving into your name. The whole lease-end process runs on the payoff letter's expiration date.

Line up the money first, pull the letter second, pay third, then follow the title. Whoever you use should do all of those, not just one.

Quick answers

How long does a lease buyout take?

Plan on four to eight weeks from the first call to a title in your name. Financing takes days to a couple of weeks, the payoff letter is good for roughly ten days, and the title release takes a few weeks after payment.

Can I buy out my lease before it ends?

Usually, but the payoff then includes the remaining payments and any early termination charge your contract names, so it costs more than waiting for the last month. Ask the leasing company for an early payoff letter to see the exact number.

Do I have to go through the dealer to buy out my lease?

That depends on the leasing company's current rule, not the dealer's. Ask the leasing company directly, in writing, who is allowed to send the payoff. If it accepts payment from you or your lender, the dealer is not needed.
