Lease Buyout Loans: Where to Get One Without Getting Worked Over

A lease buyout loan is a used-car loan with extra steps, and the steps are where every usual source falls down. Here is who writes them and what each one actually does for you.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

What a lease buyout loan is

A lease buyout loan pays the leasing company the purchase price written in your lease and puts the car in your name, with the new lender holding the lien. On the lender's own paperwork it is an ordinary used-car auto loan where the seller happens to be a leasing company.

That is the whole product. There is no special rate sheet and no special department at most lenders, and that is the problem. A buyout has parts a normal used-car purchase does not: a payoff letter with an expiration date, a purchase-option fee, sales tax charged again on the buyout price, an odometer statement, and a title that has to move out of the leasing company's name. Someone has to do each of those things. The loan does not do them. A person does, and the only real question is which person.

Who writes lease buyout loans

Four kinds of sources write most of them, and the money comes from the same places either way: banks, credit unions and finance companies. What changes is who sits between you and the money, and how much of the job they take on. The table is the short version.

The dealer-arranged loan

The dealer will arrange a loan, and it will look easy. The finance office pulls your payoff, writes a buyer's order, and sends your application out to the lenders it works with. Then it presents a rate. That rate is the rate a lender approved you at plus the dealer's compensation for placing the loan. You never see the two numbers apart.

The buyer's order is the second problem. The dealer's number is not the leasing company's number. It is the payoff plus a doc fee, an inspection of a car you already own in every way but the title, and whatever add-on products the finance office can attach. The loan is then written on that inflated figure, so you pay interest on the padding for the whole term. The four traps on the homepage cover the rest of what happens in that room.

The big bank

A big bank treats the request as a used-car loan on a car it has never seen. It pulls a book value, applies a loan-to-value cap, and compares that to your payoff. If the payoff is over the cap, the bank funds the cap and you bring the rest. Fees and sales tax are rarely financed. Then the bank hands you a check or a draft and stops. Nobody there pulls your payoff, watches the expiration date, wires the leasing company, or touches the title.

The credit union

The credit union is the big bank with a better rate and a membership form. You join, you deposit, you wait for a loan officer who sees a few of these a year. The approval carries the same loan-to-value cap and the same refusal to finance fees and tax. Some will not fund a buyout at all once they learn the seller is a leasing company, and they tend to learn it two weeks in. The rate is good. The help is none.

The big online buyout outfit

The volume shops advertise the most and explain the least. You fill in a form, the form goes out to a lender panel, and a rate comes back. The outfit's own fee, a flat processing or service charge, is rolled into the loan so you pay interest on it for the life of the term. The term is the other trick: a low payment stretched to 72 or 84 months on a car that already has three years on it. If you cannot get the same person on the phone twice, nobody there owns your file.

The small specialist

A small shop that does buyouts and nothing else works differently because it has to. It tells you the number before you apply: residual, purchase-option fee, tax, title, and where the car sits against market. It pulls the payoff and watches the clock. It finances the whole number, not the residual with a note to bring a check for the rest. It takes one application, handled privately, instead of spraying your credit across a panel. It sends the funds, then does the title, registration and plates. The full checklist is under what a real buyout partner does.

The rule. A lease buyout loan is worth exactly what the lender does after approving it. A rate attached to a to-do list is a rate on your own labor.

Why the APR is the wrong first question

Everyone asks about the rate first, and every source above knows it. The dealer buries its cut in the rate. The bank and the credit union lead with the rate because it is the only thing they are offering. The online outfit prints a rate and hides the fee.

Run the comparison the other way. Take the lender's rate and ask what is financed. If tax and fees are not, you need that cash on the payoff date, and a lower rate on a smaller loan can cost you more than a fair rate on the whole number. Ask what is in the loan that was not in your payoff letter. If a doc fee or a processing fee got financed, the rate is being charged on money you never owed. Ask who moves the title. If the answer is you, price your own time. How a rate actually gets built is in the rates guide.

Then compare. A rate a notch higher from someone who finances the tax, meets the payoff deadline and files the title is usually the cheaper loan, and it is always the finished one.

Three things to settle before anyone pulls your credit

  1. Get your own payoff letter from the leasing company. Note the amount, the purchase-option fee and the expiration date. Every quote gets measured against that sheet, not the dealer's.
  2. Ask the leasing company, in writing, who it will accept a payoff from. Some lessors restrict who can buy the car or how the funds arrive. The rules differ by company and they change, so do not take a dealer's word or a forum post for it. The third-party buyout guide explains why this decides which lenders can even help you.
  3. Know where the car sits against market value. A residual below market means equity, and equity is what gets a thinner file approved. If your credit is the worry, read the bad credit guide before you apply anywhere.

Five questions that sort lenders in five minutes

  • Will you finance the sales tax and the purchase-option fee, or just the residual?
  • Do you pull the payoff letter yourselves and pay the leasing company directly, before it expires?
  • Who handles the title transfer, lien release and registration, and what does that cost me?
  • Does my application go to one lender or to a panel, and will I be told which lenders saw it?
  • What is your fee, is it inside the loan, and can I see it in writing before I apply?

Whoever answers all five without a pause is the one to use for a car lease buyout loan.

The short version

A lease buyout loan is a used-car loan plus five pieces of work that nobody at a bank or credit union will do, and that a dealer or a volume shop will charge you to do badly.

Judge the lender by what it does, not by the APR alone. The right one tells you the number, finances all of it, meets the payoff deadline, and puts the title in your name. Anything less is a loan with homework.

Quick answers

Is a lease buyout loan the same as a used-car loan?

To the lender, yes. The difference is the work around it: a payoff letter that expires, sales tax on the buyout price, and a title moving out of the leasing company's name. Most lenders do none of that work.

Can I get a car lease buyout loan from my own bank?

Usually, if the payoff sits under the bank's book-value cap. Expect to pay the tax and fees in cash and to run the payoff, odometer statement and title yourself.

What should a lease buyout loan include?

The residual, the purchase-option fee, sales tax and the title and registration charges, in one loan. If a lender only finances the residual, the rest is due in cash on the day the payoff letter expires.
