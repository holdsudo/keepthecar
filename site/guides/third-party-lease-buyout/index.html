Third-Party Lease Buyout: Dealer vs Finance Company vs You

A leased car can be bought three ways: by you, by a dealer, or by a third party. The price, the fees and the chance the leasing company says yes are different for each.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

Three buyers, one car

The leasing company owns the car. Your lease gives you, the lessee, an option to buy it at the residual value when the term ends. That option is yours. Everything else about who can buy the car is the leasing company's call.

So there are three buyers who might show up with a check. The first is you, exercising the purchase option. The second is a dealer, usually the franchised store where you leased it or another store of the same brand. The third is anyone else: a used-car retailer, a dealer of a different brand, a friend, a stranger from a listing. When anyone in that last group pays the leasing company, it is a third party lease buyout. When the dealer does it, people call it a dealer buyout, but to the leasing company it is often just another third party with a franchise agreement.

One thing that is not a third-party buyout: a lender paying the leasing company on your behalf. If you take a loan for the buyout, you are still the buyer and the title goes into your name with the lender's lien on it. Ask the leasing company whether a lender's check for your own buyout is handled as your buyout. It normally should be, and if their answer is strange, you want to hear it before the money moves.

Why finance companies restrict third parties

The residual in your lease was set the day you signed. It was the leasing company's estimate of what the car would be worth at the end of the term. When the used market runs higher than that estimate, the car is worth more than the residual, and whoever buys it at the residual collects the difference.

The leasing company wrote the lease so that you can collect that difference. It did not write the lease so that a retailer could. If it lets any third party pay the residual and keep the car, it hands the gap to a reseller. If it refuses, the car either comes back to be sold through its own channels or goes to you. From the leasing company's chair, refusing third parties costs it nothing and keeps the upside in the family.

That is the model. What any given leasing company does with it this month is not something this page or anyone else can tell you as fact. Some accept third-party payoffs from anyone. Some accept them only from their own franchised dealers. Some accept them only from the lessee. The rule can change, and it has changed at more than one company. Ask yours for the current rule, in writing, and ask whether the third-party payoff amount is different from your own payoff amount, because sometimes it is.

The dealer buyout: two sales where one would do

Here is what happens when you let the dealer "handle" your buyout. The dealer pays the leasing company's payoff, takes the car onto its books, and then sells it to you. That is two transactions. The first is at the leasing company's price. The second is at the dealer's price, and the dealer sets it.

Between the two sales, fees get stacked. The doc fee for paperwork the leasing company's payoff package already covered. An inspection of a car the dealer leased you and you have driven for three years. Add-ons on the buyer's order. Sometimes a markup over the residual, which the dealer is entitled to try because it now owns the car. And if the dealer arranges the loan, a rate that includes the dealer's compensation for placing it. Every line is itemized in the fee guide, with a note on which ones are real.

Then the tax. Sales tax in most states is charged on the price you pay. If the dealer resells the car to you above the residual, the taxable price goes up with it. Ask your state's tax or motor vehicle agency how a buyout through a dealer is taxed where you live, because the dealer's markup can cost you twice.

"The leasing company requires it to go through us"

You will hear this. It is sometimes true for a third party, since some leasing companies route outside buyers through their dealers. For you, the lessee, the payoff letter is the rule. It will say who may pay it and how. If the letter says you can pay directly, the dealer's requirement is the dealer's preference. Read the letter before you believe the salesperson.

Finance company vs dealer: the plain difference

People search for the car lease-end buyout difference between dealer and finance company as if it were complicated. It is not. The difference is the number of hands the car passes through before it reaches you, and each pair of hands takes something.

Buying from the finance company means one payoff letter, one price, and the fees your state charges. Buying through a dealer means the same car at a price someone else chose, with a fee schedule you can read in the four traps. The finance company's letter is the cleanest document in the whole process. Start there and treat every other quote as a markup on it.

The one-line rule. If anyone other than you is buying the car from the leasing company, somebody other than you is being paid for it.

When a third party is actually the right buyer

Sometimes you do not want the car. Then a third party buyout of your car lease can be the fastest way to turn equity into cash without buying it yourself, paying tax, and selling it on. The order of operations matters: get your own payoff letter first, get the leasing company's third-party rule in writing second, and only then get offers. A retailer's offer is a reseller's number, and whether it can even be used depends on the leasing company. The retailer guide walks through the math and the blocking problem.

If the leasing company says no to third parties, the equity has not disappeared. You buy the car as the lessee, then sell it as an owner, to anyone.

How to find out the rule, in writing

  1. Call the leasing company's payoff or lease-end department, not the dealer.
  2. Ask: can I pay the payoff directly as the lessee; do you accept third-party payoffs; from whom; is the third-party amount different; does a lender's check for my own buyout count as my buyout.
  3. Ask for the answers in the payoff letter or by email. Note the letter's expiration date and the per-day charge after it.
  4. If you need a loan, line it up against that letter, not against the dealer's number. The loan guide covers who will fund the whole buyout, tax included, and who will not.

The short version

You are the buyer the lease was written for. Buying from the finance company at the residual, plus the state's tax and title, is the cheapest version of this transaction and the only one where nobody else sets the price.

A dealer buyout is the same car sold twice, with fees at each step. A third-party buyout is allowed only when the leasing company says so, and its offer is a reseller's number. Get the rule in writing, get your own payoff letter, and let every other quote explain itself against that.

Quick answers

What is a third-party lease buyout?

It is when someone other than the lessee pays the leasing company's payoff and takes the car, such as a used-car retailer or a dealer of another brand. The leasing company decides whether to allow it, so ask for its current rule in writing.

Why does a dealer buyout cost more than buying from the finance company?

Because the dealer buys the car first and resells it to you at its own price. That adds a doc fee, an inspection, add-ons, possibly a markup over the residual, and a loan rate with the dealer's compensation inside it.

Is a loan from my own lender a third-party buyout?

No. You remain the buyer and the title goes in your name with the lender's lien on it. Confirm with the leasing company that a lender's check for your own buyout is treated as your buyout.
