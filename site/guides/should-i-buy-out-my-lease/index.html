Should I Buy Out My Lease, Trade It In, or Return It?

Three doors at the end of a lease: buy it, trade it, return it. One of them keeps your equity, one hands it to the dealer, and one pays the leasing company to take the car back.

Published October 19, 2026 · bestleaseend.com

Start with the only question that matters

Typing should I buyout my lease into a search box gets you a hundred opinions. The answer is a comparison, not an opinion. Is the car worth more than it costs you to keep it?

The cost to keep it is your buyout: residual, purchase-option fee, your state's tax and title. The calculation guide walks through each line. What the car is worth is the private-party value for your year, trim and mileage, from the pricing guides, not from the dealer. The gap between those two numbers is your equity, and the sign of that gap decides most of this.

Say the payoff is $18,300 and a private buyer would pay $22,000. That is $3,700 of equity, and every door except one gives some or all of it away. Say instead the car is worth $16,000. Now you are being asked to pay $2,300 more than the car is worth to keep it, and the honest answer may be no. Those numbers are invented. Run yours.

What tips it toward buying

  • Positive equity. The car is worth more than the payoff. Buying it is the only way to keep that money.
  • Mileage overage. If you are over the allowance, returning the car means paying per mile. Buying it means nobody counts.
  • Wear and damage. The return inspection bills you for every scuff beyond "normal." A purchase has no inspection. The wear is yours and nobody charges you for it.
  • You know the car. Its service history, its habits, its accident record, which is none. The used market cannot sell you that certainty at any price.
  • You would keep it for years. A buyout you finance over a few years and then drive payment-free for several more is the cheapest car you will ever own. A buyout you flip in eighteen months is a used-car purchase with extra steps.
  • The repair outlook is dull. A car that has needed nothing but oil and tires is a car worth keeping past the warranty.

What tips it toward returning

Returning the car is right more often than a buyout site would like to admit. Here is when.

  • Negative equity, and no overages. If the car is worth well under the payoff and you are inside your mileage with clean wear, the leasing company guessed high on the residual. That is its problem. Hand it back and let it absorb the loss.
  • The car has been trouble. Repeated repairs under warranty become repeated repairs out of your pocket. Do not buy a known problem because it is familiar.
  • Big maintenance is due. Tires, brakes, a timing service. Price them. If they land in the first year of ownership, add them to the buyout.
  • You do not want it. Your life changed. The car does not fit. Equity is a reason to buy and resell, not a reason to keep driving something wrong.
  • The loan is bad. If the only rate you can get is ugly, it can erase the equity over the term. The lease-then-buyout guide shows how the math moves with the rate.

Returning is not free. You pay the disposition fee, any mileage overage and any wear charges. If you have negative equity and heavy overages at the same time, compare the two bills: the overages you would pay to return against the gap you would overpay to buy. Sometimes buying a car that is slightly underwater is cheaper than returning it with a large mileage bill. Do that arithmetic once, on paper, before anyone else does it for you.

Trade-in: handing the equity to the dealer

The dealer's favorite door. You bring the leased car in, the dealer "handles the buyout," and you leave in a new one. What happens underneath: the dealer pays the leasing company's payoff, keeps the car, and resells it at retail. Whatever gap existed between the payoff and the car's value is now the dealer's.

If the dealer is generous, it shows you some of that gap as "equity" toward the new deal. But the dealer sets the appraisal, the dealer sets the price of the new car, and the dealer sets the new payment. You see none of those numbers independently. The question buyout lease or trade in usually answers itself: trade-in is the one option where you never see the math.

Two more problems. A dealer of a different brand may not be allowed to pay off your lease at all, because to your leasing company it is a third party. And if you are underwater, the dealer rolls the shortfall into the new lease, where you pay it again with finance charges. Selling the car to a retailer has the same restriction problem, covered in the retailer guide.

The one-line rule. If the car is worth more than the payoff, every door except buying it hands that difference to someone else.

The last check: the rate and the term

A good buyout can be ruined by a bad loan. The dealer will offer one with its compensation inside the rate. A big bank will cap the loan at book value and leave you paying tax and fees in cash. A credit union will give you a rate and nothing else, on a payoff letter that expires while the loan officer is on vacation. The list of what a real buyout partner does is the test to apply to anyone offering the loan.

Watch the term as closely as the rate. A buyout stretched to six or seven years on a car that already has three years on it is the dealer's oldest trick in a new wrapper. Shorter is better, and payment-free years at the end are the whole point.

So, should you buyout a leased car? If it has equity, it has been reliable, you would keep it, and you can get a sane loan: yes, and quickly, because the payoff letter has a date on it. If it is underwater, troubled, or simply not the car you want: return it, pay the fees, and do not let anyone talk you into buying it to "protect your equity" that is not there.

The short version

Buy the car when it is worth more than the payoff, it has been reliable, you would keep it, and the loan is sane. That is the one door that keeps the equity with you and skips every return charge.

Return it when it is underwater with clean miles, or when it has been trouble, or when you do not want it. Never trade it in to find out which; the trade-in is the only option where the dealer sees the math and you do not.

Quick answers

Is it worth buying out a lease?

It is worth it when the car's private-party value is above the payoff, the car has been reliable, and you plan to keep it. If the car is worth less than the payoff and you are inside your mileage, returning it usually costs less.

Can I trade in a leased car?

Dealers do it all the time. The dealer pays off the leasing company and keeps whatever equity exists, showing you only what it chooses as credit toward a new deal. A different brand's dealer may not be allowed to pay off your lease at all.

Should I return my lease if I am over the mileage?

Compare the mileage and wear bill against the gap between payoff and market value. If buying leaves you only slightly underwater and returning costs a large overage charge, buying can be the cheaper way out.
